Two Stacks,
One Roofline

How a JAX program becomes TPU instructions, how a PyTorch program becomes GPU instructions, why the two ended up shaped so differently, and the four pieces of arithmetic that predicted every number this study measured.

1 · The two stacks, layer by layer

One compiles the whole program ahead of time. The other calls fast libraries and compiles when asked.

TPU

jax → stablehlo → xla:tpu → libtpu
JAX / FlaxPython, functional, pure. Or PyTorch via torchax, which is a torch API on JAX.
↓ trace
jaxprA flat, typed, side-effect-free graph. Shapes are static here, which is the whole bargain.
↓ lower

↓ compile

↓ escape hatch

↓ serving

NVIDIA

PyTorchEager by default. Every op dispatches immediately, which is why the model is easy to debug and slow by default.
↓ optional

vLLM (native)pip install vllm works. This is the difference the market notices.

The structural difference in one sentence. On TPU the compiler makes the performance decisions and there are few knobs, so the floor is high and the ceiling is the compiler's. On GPU the user makes them and there are many knobs, so the floor is lower and the ceiling is whoever writes the best kernel. That is why Unsloth exists on GPU and has no TPU equivalent: it is a project that beats a default, and the TPU default is already 84.2% of peak on a dense matmul, measured here.

2 · The roofline

Every kernel is either short of the flat part or short of the slope

One inequality governs all of it. A kernel doing W FLOPs while moving Q bytes has arithmetic intensity I = W/Q, and its achievable rate is bounded by whichever runs out first, the arithmetic units or the memory system.

P ≤ min( Ppeak , I × B )
Ppeak = 918 TFLOP/s bf16 on v6e. B = 1,638 GB/s of HBM. The corner, where the two terms meet, sits at I* = Ppeak/B ≈ 560 FLOPs per byte. Above that intensity a kernel can reach peak; below it, it cannot, no matter how well written.

Why the gather is not merely slow but off the model. A row gather does one useful transfer per index, so I ≈ 1, which the roofline says should give about 1,638 GB/s ÷ 1 ≈ its bandwidth limit. We measured 1.3 GB/s, 0.079% of that. The roofline assumes streaming access, and the memory system moves a wide burst per transaction. Ask for 4 scattered bytes and the DRAM still moves its granule, so the useful fraction of each transaction collapses. This is the one case where the honest model is not the roofline but the transaction: effective B = B × (useful bytes / granule).

And a granule effect explains our other negative result. We predicted sorting the indices would recover bandwidth by improving locality, and measured no change at all (×1.00 across random, sorted, blocked and sequential). So on this generation the penalty is not paid at the row level where sorting would help.

3 · Bisection bandwidth

The one number that explained our whole scaling curve

Cut the network in half. Count the links crossing the cut. That is the bisection, and in an all-to-all every chip must send roughly half its data to the far side, so the cut is the bottleneck no scheduling can route around.

Bbisect = 2 · min(X, Y) · blink
tall-to-all ≈ (N/2 · D) / Bbisect
A 2D torus of X×Y chips, each link carrying blink. The factor 2 is the wraparound: a torus cut severs the links in both directions. D is the payload each chip holds.

Now substitute Google's published v6e topologies, and the measurement falls out:

The ratio doubles exactly once, from 4×4 to 4×8, because the torso grows along one axis while the cut does not. The measurement doubles exactly once too, by 1.77×. After that the ratio is constant and the measurement is flat to within 4% across an eightfold increase in chips. This is the study's central result and it is arithmetic, not a fit.

The prediction came before the data: the 4×8 anomaly was proposed as bisection congestion, and the 256-chip burst then measured the flatness the model requires. This is also precisely what TPU 8i attacks by dropping the torus for a high-radix optical fabric with a diameter of 7 hops instead of 16.

4 · Latency hiding

Why a 0.027 ms operation took 0.55 ms

Our first collective numbers were wrong by 20× because each measurement paid a host dispatch cost. The fix was to chain operations inside one compiled call, and the arithmetic for how many is Little's law.

n ≥ L / top
To hide a latency L behind work of duration top, keep at least n operations in flight. Measured: L ≈ 0.53 ms of dispatch, top ≈ 0.027 ms, so n ≈ 20. Below that the host is the bottleneck and the fabric is idle.

This is not a benchmarking curiosity. It is the reason CUDA Graphs exist on the GPU side, and the reason TPU 8i deletes four SparseCores and spends the area on a Collectives Acceleration Engine that cuts on-chip collective latency 5×. At small message sizes the network was never the problem; the orchestration was. Our measured ratio, 20 to 1, is the size of the prize.

5 · The KV cache crossover

The arithmetic that split the industry's silicon in two

Weights are a fixed cost. The KV cache is not: it grows with context and batch, and there is a context length past which the cache is larger than the model.

Mweights = P · s
Mkv = 2 · Lattn · hkv · dhead · s · C · b
P parameters at s bytes each. Then 2 for K and V, over Lattn attention layers, hkv key-value heads of width dhead, context C, batch b. Grouped-query attention is the hkv term, and it is why a 671B model can be served at all.
C* = P / (2 · Lattn · hkv · dhead · b)
Set the two equal and solve. For Qwen3-4B at batch 32, C* ≈ 13k tokens: past that, KV exceeds weights. At C = 262k the model holds 8 GB of weights and 155 GB of KV, a ratio of 19 to 1. Computed by capacity_calculator.py in this repo.

Read that ratio and the 2027 roadmaps stop being marketing. Prefill is compute-bound and gets Rubin CPX with cheap GDDR7. Decode is bandwidth-bound and gets VR200 with HBM4. Google splits the same seam: 8t keeps SparseCore and the torus for training, while 8i takes 288 GB of HBM and 384 MB of on-chip SRAM so long-context KV stops spilling. Two competitors, one equation, the same conclusion in the same year.

None of this arithmetic is new. Bisection bandwidth and Little's law predate the hardware by decades, and the roofline is twenty years old. That is the argument for learning them rather than learning a chip: v6e will be superseded in 2027 and these five equations will not be.
