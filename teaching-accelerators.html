A teaching page · August 2026

Where Does
the Data Live?

Seven companies are building AI accelerators and they look nothing alike. But every one of them is answering the same question, and once you know the question the whole zoo sorts itself into a single line. This page is that line, plus the practical business of getting thirty students onto real hardware.

1 · The question every accelerator answers

Arithmetic is cheap. Moving the operands is not.

A modern accelerator can multiply far faster than it can be fed. On a TPU v6e the ratio is stark and public: 918 TFLOP/s of arithmetic against 1,638 GB/s of memory bandwidth. Divide them and you get the number that governs everything.

I* = Ppeak / B = 918e12 / 1638e9 ≈ 560 FLOPs per byte

A kernel must do 560 arithmetic operations for every byte it loads, or the memory system is the limit and the multipliers idle. Almost nothing in a transformer naturally clears 560 except a large matmul.

So every accelerator design is a bet about where to put the data so the multipliers stay fed. That is the single axis the whole industry varies along, and it is the right way to teach the zoo:

Which stack lets an enterprise scale? Rank by switching cost, not by peak FLOPs, and the answer is uncomfortable for everyone except NVIDIA.

  1. NVIDIA wins on ecosystem gravity: every kernel, every serving framework and every new model lands here first. pip install vllm works.
  2. TPU wins on price per unit of work if you are already in JAX, and loses badly on day-one friction. We hit six independent install walls in one day, documented in the companion log. That friction is why no third party serves on TPU.
  3. Trainium is the same architectural bet as TPU with AWS distribution, so its adoption question is identical: how much JAX-or-Neuron rewriting will you tolerate.
  4. Groq and Cerebras are not adoption decisions at all, they are API or appliance decisions. You cannot port to them; you call them.
  5. AMD is the live question of 2026-27. MI455X's claimed 19.6 TB/s is the first credible bandwidth lead over NVIDIA, and ROCm's vLLM support is now real. If a migration is going to happen, it happens here first.

Teach this before any vendor slide. A student who knows the 560 number can look at any new chip announcement and immediately ask the only question that matters: did they raise the arithmetic, the bandwidth, or the distance? Almost every 2027 part on the next table raised the last two.

The pattern to make students find for themselves. Give them that table with the "role" column blanked and ask what changed between 2024 and 2027. The answer is that the industry split the chip by phase. NVIDIA separated prefill from decode; Google separated training from inference. Two competitors, different fabs, same year, same conclusion. Then ask why, and the answer is on the next section.

Note for honesty in lectures: Groq's 80 TB/s and Cerebras' 125 PFLOPS are real but come with a hard constraint, which is that the model must fit in the SRAM or on the wafer. Quote peak numbers only alongside the constraint that makes them achievable.

3 · From the transformer to the hardware

Four operations, four different bottlenecks

"Attention Is All You Need" is eight pages of maths with no mention of memory. Read it again with the 560 number in hand and every hardware decision of the last decade follows.

operationcostarithmetic intensitybottleneckhardware answer
FFN matmulO(L·d²)high, ≫560computesystolic arrays, tensor cores. This is the easy part.
Attention scoresO(L²·d)falls with Lmemory at long LFlashAttention, Splash Attention: never materialise the L×L matrix
KV cache readO(L·d) per token≈ 1bandwidthHBM4, 384 MB of on-chip SRAM on 8i, paged attention
MoE routingO(tokens) all-to-all≈ 0networkcollectives engines, 7-hop fabrics, SparseCore

The crossover worth putting on the board

C* = P / (2 · Lattn · hkv · dhead · b)

The context length at which the KV cache equals the weights. For a 4B model at batch 32, C* ≈ 13k tokens. At 262k context that model holds 8 GB of weights and 155 GB of KV cache, a ratio of 19 to 1.

That single ratio explains prefill/decode disaggregation, 8i's SRAM, HBM being sold out through 2026, and why Groq's all-SRAM bet exists at all. It is the most valuable equation to hand a class, because they can derive the industry's roadmap from it in one lecture.

4 · The lesson no textbook contains

Quota is not capacity, and both are not availability

Students will spend more time failing to get a machine than failing to write a kernel. Teach the distinction explicitly, because the three failures look identical in a terminal and have opposite correct responses.

failurewhat it sayscorrect response
quotaQuota exceeded, or a grant of literally 0Retrying is useless. Change zone, or ask for a grant.
capacityno more capacity in the zoneRetrying is exactly right; capacity churns by the minute.
permissiondoes not have permission for accelerator type, or Required compute.instances.create permissionAn IAM or per-type grant problem. Only an admin fixes it, and retrying anywhere will not help.

Measured on this project, 16 August 2026. A single class project, nine zones probed hourly for a v6e-8:

zoneverdictnote
us-east1-davailablethe only reliable multi-chip zone we found
asia-northeast1-bavailablealso worked, higher latency to everything else
us-east5-a / -bno capacityrefusals took 61s and 213s: admitted, then rolled back
us-east5-cquotaa grant of zero. No amount of retrying helps.
us-south1-ano capacityrefused in 7s: an instant, honest no
us-central1-ano capacity
europe-west4-ano capacity

Three gates, not one. Quota, capacity and permission are independent, and a number on a quota page is evidence about none of the others. Measured on this one project in a single day: v6e spot had all three; v6e on-demand had quota but no permission in the zone that had capacity; us-east5 had permission but no capacity; and the GPU quota of 32 A100s turned out to have no compute.instances.create permission at all, so those GPUs were never usable. Teach students to read the error, not the quota page.

Two more facts worth telling them on day one. First, paying more does not conjure chips: an on-demand request at 1.92× the spot price was refused in the same zone with the same capacity error. Second, the zone matters more than the quota: this project holds a grant for 512 chips and could obtain 32.

5 · Thirty students on one cluster

The GKE arrangement that keeps one student from eating the class

The failure mode is predictable: one student's notebook holds eight chips for a weekend while twenty-nine wait. Four Kubernetes pieces prevent it, and they are worth teaching as infrastructure literacy rather than hiding behind a helper script.

  1. A namespace per student. The unit of isolation, and the thing RBAC binds to.
  2. A ResourceQuota per namespace. Hard caps on accelerator count, pods and storage. Without this, nothing else matters.
  3. Kueue for queuing. A ClusterQueue per student inside one cohort, so idle quota is borrowed rather than wasted. This is the piece that turns a first-come free-for-all into a fair queue, and it is what makes a 30-student class work on hardware sized for 8.
  4. Taints and PriorityClasses. Taint accelerator nodes so CPU-only pods cannot squat on them, and give graded assignments a higher priority class than exploration.

Sketch for a class of 30

« node pools »
  cpu-pool          e2-standard-8, autoscaling 0-30   # notebooks, editors, data prep
  tpu-pool          v6e-8, 2 node pools of 2 hosts    # GKE treats a multi-host slice as ATOMIC:
                                                      # hosts = nodes in the pool, all or nothing
  gpu-pool          a2-highgpu-1g, autoscaling 0-4    # the NVIDIA half of the course

« per student »
  namespace         student-NN
  ResourceQuota     requests.google.com/tpu: 8
                    count/pods: 10
  LocalQueue        student-NN  ->  ClusterQueue student-NN

« one cohort, so idle quota flows »
  ClusterQueue student-NN
    cohort: class-cohort
    nominalQuota: 8 chips        # what they are guaranteed
    borrowingLimit: 24 chips     # what they may take when others are idle
    preemption: reclaimWithinCohort=Any

« multi-host TPU jobs »
  JobSet + Kueue                 # a multi-host slice must be scheduled as one unit
  taint: google.com/tpu=present:NoSchedule

The one non-obvious thing. GKE treats a multi-host TPU slice as atomic: the number of hosts equals the number of nodes in the pool and the scheduler gives you all of it or none. That is why JobSet exists, and why a student cannot get "half a slice" the way they can get half a GPU node.

Practical order of setup: reserve capacity in one zone first, because none of the above helps if the zone is empty. Then node pools, then namespaces and quotas, then Kueue, then hand out kubeconfigs. Test the whole thing with two fake students before thirty real ones arrive.

6 · What to assign

Assignments where the hardware teaches the lesson, not the slides

Each of these produced a real result in this study, which is the argument that they are worth a student's week. Each also fails informatively, which matters more.

  1. Find the roofline corner yourself. Measure a matmul and a gather. Expect 84% of peak and 0.08% of bandwidth on the same chip, and explain the four-order gap.
  2. Measure a collective, then find your own harness bug. Time an all-reduce naively, then chain 32 of them inside one compiled call. The number moves by 20×, and the difference is host dispatch, not the network.
  3. Predict the topology from timings. Measure all-to-all at 8, 16 and 32 chips, then reconstruct the bisection width. Check it against the published topology afterwards.
  4. Compute C* for a model you like, then verify it by watching memory at increasing context until KV overtakes weights.
  5. Dense against MoE at matched active FLOPs. Same arithmetic, different movement. The gap is the routing tax, and it is the reason 8i exists.
  6. Port one kernel to two vendors. Same maths in Pallas and in Triton. The lesson is which layer owns the decisions, and it lands harder than any lecture on it.

Grade the verification, not the number. Three of this study's own measurements were artifacts: a chained-op timing that had not converged, a sub-mesh whose timing process owned no chips in the mesh, and a gather sweep that was non-monotonic. All three were caught by checks, not by cleverness. A student who reports a wrong number with the check that would have caught it has learned more than one who reports a right number by luck.
