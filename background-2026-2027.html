In 2026 both Google and NVIDIA stopped building one chip. They split their silicon in half, by phase, and the reason is the same on both sides: the expensive part of an AI workload is no longer the multiplying. It is the moving.

Everything on this page is dated. Where a number came from a measurement on a live TPU v6e rather than a vendor slide, it says so.

1 · The money, so the rest is not abstract

Capital expenditure crossed the point where architecture is decided by economics

The four large hyperscalers are spending roughly $725 to $760 billion on infrastructure in 2026, up about 77% from around $410 billion in 2025, and analysts at Evercore and Bank of America both project the 2027 figure passes one trillion. Alphabet alone guided to $175 to $185 billion for 2026, roughly double its $91.4 billion in 2025.

Google projects 4.3 million TPUs shipped in 2026, 10 million in 2027, and more than 35 million in 2028. Anthropic's commitment alone reaches 3.5 gigawatts of compute coming online in 2027. At that volume, an architectural choice is not a preference. It is a multi-billion dollar bet on what the workload will be.

2 · Google's split

Two chips, and the difference is what they do with the data-movement budget

Announced at Cloud Next in April 2026, both on TSMC 2nm, both targeted at late 2027. Ironwood, the seventh generation, went generally available at the same event.

The detail that gives the game away. On 8i, four SparseCores are removed and a Collectives Acceleration Engine is put in their place, cutting on-chip collective latency by 5×. Google spent inference transistors on a unit whose entire job is moving data between chips. Not one extra matrix unit.

3 · NVIDIA's split

The same bet, arrived at independently, with different letters on it

Vera Rubin entered full production in June 2026. The interesting part is not the flagship, it is that NVIDIA also broke inference in half: Rubin CPX handles prefill, VR200 NVL72 handles decode, and they are deliberately different silicon.

An NVL144 CPX rack reaches 8 exaflops, 100 TB of fast memory and 1.7 PB/s of aggregate bandwidth at about 370 kW, roughly 7.5× a GB300 NVL72. Rubin Ultra follows in 2027 in a 600 kW rack; Feynman in 2028 brings co-packaged optics, which is the same admission again: the wire is the problem.

Two companies, different customers, different fabs, different decades of institutional habit, and both concluded in the same year that prefill and decode want different chips. When competitors converge like that, they are not copying. They are reading the same profile.

4 · The workload that forced it

A mixture-of-experts token spends its life in transit

Routing a token through an MoE layer is: score the experts, take the top-k, permute tokens by expert, dispatch them across chips, run a modest amount of matrix maths, gather the outputs back, and reduce. Six of those seven steps are data movement.

On Google's silicon the permute, dispatch, gather and reduce follow the same gather/scatter/reduce pattern as the embedding pipeline, which is what SparseCore was built for, with its sort support covering the top-k. That is why the routing question and the recommender question turn out to be the same hardware question.

5 · The number that explains Boardfly

Sixteen hops become seven

In a 3D torus, any chip can eventually reach any other, but a worst-case crossing on Ironwood takes up to 16 hops. Fine when gradients move once per step. Ruinous when every token may need any chip. So 8i drops the torus for a high-radix design with optical circuit switches, and caps the diameter at seven.

6 · Measured here, today

Irregular access on current silicon runs at 0.08% of the memory it is attached to

On a live TPU v6e, gathering 1 million indices out of a 4.2 million row table takes 6.25 ms on the TensorCore path. That is about 1.3 GB/s effective, against roughly 1,600 GB/s of HBM bandwidth sitting right there on the package.

HBM bandwidth available · 1,600 GB/s100%
Achieved by a TensorCore gather · 1.3 GB/s0.08%

The second bar is not a rendering error. That gap is the reason SparseCore exists, the reason 8i grew a collectives engine, the reason NVIDIA and Sakana spent ICML 2026 on tile-wise sparse formats, and the reason a network diameter got cut in half. Every roadmap item on this page is a different attempt to close the same gap.

Measured 16 August 2026 on a v6e-1 in us-east5-a, JAX 0.10.2, single chip. The SparseCore path via jax.experimental.pallas.tpu_sc is the comparison worth running next, and the documentation claims 4 to 5×.

7 · The research pattern underneath

An agent in a loop with something that can tell it that it is wrong

The other convergence of 2025 and 2026 is methodological. Everyone doing serious optimisation work has built the same machine: a language model proposes, a real evaluator scores, and compute does the searching.

The method is commoditised. The evaluator is scarce. Anyone can call a model. Almost nobody can score its output against a real SparseCore, and almost nobody scoring EDA output holds a commercial sign-off licence. The gap between 1.36× and 2.08× in that table is an open problem with a public scoreboard.

8 · Where the public money went

The labs bought the same bet

The Department of Energy, NVIDIA and Oracle are building Solstice, 100,000 Blackwell GPUs, alongside Equinox at 10,000 GPUs delivering in 2026, both at Argonne, for a combined 2,200 exaflops of AI performance, wired into experimental facilities like the Advanced Photon Source. Argonne has three more systems planned: Tara, Minerva and Janus. Nine new DOE supercomputers were announced in total.

This matters for anyone aiming at a lab: the people who will run those machines are hiring for the ability to model a machine before it exists, because that is how procurement decisions get made. Calibrating a performance model on hardware you have and projecting it onto hardware you do not is not a student exercise. It is the job.

9 · The other scarce evaluator

These chips were designed with tools that sit on a Stanford EE licence

TPU 8t was designed with Broadcom, 8i with MediaTek, both on TSMC 2nm. That work runs through the same commercial flows a Stanford EE student can log into: Cadence Genus and Innovus, Synopsys synthesis and place-and-route, Siemens Catapult for high-level synthesis. The tools are not the bottleneck for a student. Access to them plus real compute is, and that combination is rare enough to be a research position in itself.

The live example is macro placement. AlphaChip is still contested in 2026 rather than settled: Synopsys's VP for AI and ML said publicly that reinforcement learning in core EDA algorithms "hasn't really panned out", neither Google nor Ricursive has published results on modern public benchmarks, and the UCSD and TILOS MacroPlacement project had to build the Cadence Genus and Innovus reference flows itself because the commercial baseline was the missing piece. The argument does not resolve because the people with the reinforcement learning rarely have tool baselines, and the people with the tools rarely have the compute to train the agent.

Same shape as section 7. A commercial sign-off tool is an evaluator: slow, expensive, authoritative, and exactly what an agent needs to be told it is wrong by. The MLCAD 2026 contest was already an agentic LLM flow scored on timing optimisation. That contest closed in June 2026; the next windows are MLCAD 2027 and ISPD 2027 in Taipei, 31 March to 2 April, with calls usually out in late 2026.

One practical caution before any of this is published: commercial EDA licences typically restrict publishing benchmark results. MacroPlacement navigates this carefully. Ask the department's CAD administrator in writing first, and if the answer is no, results can still be reported against "a leading commercial tool".

10 · What follows from all of it

Three things are true at once, and they point the same way

  1. The bottleneck is irregular data movement, and both vendors just spent their 2027 transistor budget saying so.
  2. The tooling to study it without owning the chip exists and is current: ASTRA-sim 3.0 for collectives, SCALE-Sim for systolic arrays, Calculon for analytical co-design, StableHLO-based cross-architecture modelling from April 2026. All of them are calibrated mostly against NVIDIA GPUs, and none of them model SparseCore.
  3. The agent-plus-evaluator loop turns compute into results, and the scoreboard for TPU kernels is public and unfinished.

Which leaves an unusually well-shaped opening: measure the irregular primitives on a v6e, including the SparseCore path nobody has benchmarked publicly, use them to calibrate the open simulators that currently guess at TPUs, and point the resulting model at the one workload the whole industry just redesigned itself around.
